# Shush — What We're Building (Plain English)

> A review document. No technical detail — this describes what a person using Shush
> actually sees and does. Every decision here is settled; nothing is left open.

---

## 1. What Shush is, in one paragraph

Shush is a website where you can start talking to a stranger who shares your interests
within a few seconds of arriving — no sign-up, no form, no email. You pick a couple of
things you're interested in, we give you a name, and we find you someone. You talk. If it
goes well, you can ask to keep them — and if they agree, they're in your friends list and
you can come back to them any time. If you want to make sure you don't lose all that, you
can save your account with an email whenever you like, and everything comes with you.

---

## 2. The principles behind the decisions

1. **Talking comes first.** A new visitor is in a conversation within about three clicks.
   Nothing is asked that isn't needed to make a match.
2. **Nothing is asked twice.** No email confirmation, no profile setup, no "tell us about
   yourself." If we can decide something for you, we do.
3. **You're anonymous by default, and that's a real choice, not a trial.** The anonymous
   experience is complete. Signing up protects what you have; it doesn't unlock the basics.
4. **You meet people by being matched, never by browsing.** There's no directory, no
   search, no list of strangers to pick through.
5. **A conversation is with one person at a time.** Like a real conversation.
6. **You always know where you stand.** If your account can only be reached from this
   browser, we say so plainly rather than letting you find out by losing it.

## 3. The complete journey, start to finish

### First visit

**Step 1 — You land on the page.**
A short line explaining what this is, and one button: **Start chatting**. Nothing else
competing for attention.

**Step 2 — You pick what you're into.**
One screen, five interest tiles. Tap one or two, and **Continue** lights up.

A first-time visitor sees the five most popular interests. A returning visitor sees the
ones they picked last time, already there. A small **show others** link opens the full
list for anyone who wants something different, but most people never need it.

**Step 3 — You get a name.**
We assign one automatically — something like *Quiet Otter*, *Amber Fern*, *Blue Marlin* —
along with a **shuffle** button. Shuffle as many times as you like; you'll always get a
name nobody else is using. You never type a username, never check if one is taken, never
pick a password.

That's the third click.

**Step 4 — You choose how long you're willing to wait.**
Three options, right above the **Find someone** button:

| Option | What it means |
|---|---|
| **5 seconds** *(default)* | Try to find someone who shares my interests. If nobody turns up in 5 seconds, just give me anyone. |
| **10 seconds** | Same, but try a bit harder before settling. |
| **Wait as long as it takes** | Only match me with someone who actually shares my interests, however long that takes. |

This is a speed-versus-quality dial, and it's honest about the trade. When Shush is quiet,
the 5-second option will usually hand you a random person — which is the point of it.

**Step 5 — You're matched, and you talk.**
The conversation opens with the interests you have in common shown at the top, so you can
see why you were put together — or a note that this was a random match if the wait ran out.

You can send text and photos. You can see when the other person is typing, and when
they've read what you sent.

**If they lose connection mid-conversation, you're told** — a quiet line saying they've
gone offline. The conversation stays open, because they might come back, and anything you
send while they're away reaches them when they do, in the right order.

**If they deliberately leave, you're told that too**, and the conversation is over.

### Keeping someone

**Step 6 — You ask to keep them.**
There's an **Add friend** button in every conversation. Tap it and they get a request.

You can send it while you're still talking, or right after the conversation ends. It's the
one thing that still works after someone has left — it appears as the last item in that
conversation, so a good chat is never lost just because someone closed their laptop first.

**What happens next:**

- **They accept** → they're in your friends list, the conversation is kept with its full
  history, and either of you can pick it up any time.
- **They decline** → nothing is announced. You simply never hear back.
- **Nobody sends a request** → the conversation and its messages are deleted. Strangers
  stay strangers.

This is deliberate: **conversations only survive if at least one person wanted them to.**

**Step 7 — We ask you to save your account.**
This is the *only* moment we ask, and we ask it right after your first friend request is
accepted — the first moment you have something you'd be sad to lose.

The message is honest: *"Right now, the only thing that connects you to Quiet Otter is
this browser. Clear your history or pick up your phone instead, and your friends are gone
— and they can't find you either. Want to save it?"*

You give an email and a password. Your name, your friends, your conversations and their
history carry over exactly as they are. Nothing resets. Signing up also lets you replace
your assigned name with one you choose.

If you say no, you carry on. A small, non-blocking reminder stays in the corner.

### Coming back

**Step 8 — You return.**
If you saved your account, sign in from any device and everything is there. If you didn't,
and you're on the same browser, everything is still there too.

You see your friends list, **who's online right now**, and how many unread messages are
waiting from each of them.

**Step 9 — You keep going.**
Open a friend and pick up where you left off, or find someone new — same flow as before,
minus the name step, with your last interests already selected.

**One conversation at a time.** Opening a friend closes whatever you had open. If you were
talking to a stranger, that conversation ends — strangers don't wait around. Friends do:
their conversation is exactly where you left it, and anything they sent while you were
away is waiting.

## 4. The features, listed out

### Getting started
- Start chatting with no sign-up at all
- Five interest tiles — the popular ones for newcomers, your own for returning visitors
- Full interest list available behind a **show others** link
- Automatic name assignment with unlimited shuffling
- Choose your matching patience: 5 seconds, 10 seconds, or wait indefinitely
- Your identity persists on the same browser without an account

### Chatting
- One-to-one conversations, one at a time
- Text messages
- Photos — including with strangers
- Shared interests shown at the top of every conversation
- See when someone is typing
- See when your message has been read
- **Be told when the other person goes offline mid-conversation**
- **Be told when the other person leaves for good**
- Messages sent to someone who's away are delivered when they return
- Messages always arrive in the order they were sent
- You never see the same message twice, even on a flaky connection
- Scroll back through the whole conversation

### People
- Send a friend request during a conversation, or after it has ended
- Accept or decline requests you receive
- A friends list you can open any time
- **See which of your friends are online right now**
- Unread message counts per friend
- Share an invite link so someone you know can talk to you directly
- Block someone
- Report someone

### Your account
- Save your anonymous account with an email whenever you like
- Everything transfers — name, friends, conversations, history
- Choose your own name once you've signed up
- Sign in from any device
- Sign out

### Look and feel
- Dark mode and light mode
- **Dark mode is the default**
- Your choice is remembered

## 5. Why anyone would sign up

The anonymous experience is complete, so signing up has to protect something real. It
does, and these are in order of how much they actually matter:

**1. Your friends disappear with your browser.** This is the real one. An anonymous
identity is remembered by the browser you're using, and browsers genuinely forget — you
clear your history, you use private browsing, your phone tidies up storage it hasn't
touched in a while, you get a new laptop. On that day you don't just lose a name, you lose
every person you'd connected with, permanently. They can't reach you either. People
understand that loss immediately.

**2. You're stuck on one device.** Started talking on your phone at lunch and want to
carry on from your laptop? You can't, unless you've signed up.

**3. You can pick your own name.** Assigned names are fine, but *Quiet Otter* is only ever
assigned. A name you choose is something only a saved account can have.

**4. Photos expire in anonymous conversations.** Images shared where neither person has an
account are deleted after 24 hours. With an account they're kept for 30 days. Worth being
plain about: storing images costs real money, so this is a genuine cost difference rather
than an invented restriction.

**The timing matters more than the wording.** We don't ask on arrival — that would break
principle 1 to sell something they have no reason to want yet. We ask the moment a friend
request is accepted, when the account stops being empty.

**No artificial limits.** There's no "only 3 friends without an account," no locked
features. The reasons above are enough, and invented gates would make the product feel
manipulative. Photo expiry is the single exception, because it reflects a real cost.

## 6. Why there's no searching for people by name

Three reasons:

1. **The names are random, so searching them is meaningless.** Nobody goes looking for
   "Amber Fern" — they wouldn't know to.
2. **It's the main tool for harassment.** If someone leaves a conversation because they're
   uncomfortable, name search lets the other person find them again straight away.
   Without it, leaving actually works.
3. **It fights the premise.** Shush is about being matched with people, not looking up
   people you already know.

The one genuine need underneath the question — *"come talk to me on Shush"* — is handled by
the **invite link**. You generate a link, send it to whoever you like however you like, and
they land straight in a conversation with you. Deliberate, opt-in on both sides, and
useless for trawling.

## 7. How names are made — and why not with AI

Two word lists: roughly 200 adjectives and 200 nouns, combined into *Quiet Otter*, *Amber
Fern*, *Rapid Cedar*. That's 40,000 combinations, and adding a number when one is taken
makes it effectively unlimited. This is how Docker names containers and how GitHub
suggests repository names.

**The cost of doing it with AI, since you asked:** roughly ₹0.02 per name — about ₹220 for
10,000 users. Money isn't the objection. The objections are:

- **It's slow.** Half a second or more, landing right inside the three-click flow.
- **It can fail.** If the service is down, a new visitor stares at a spinner on their very
  first screen.
- **It can produce something offensive.** Two curated lists physically cannot — you control
  every possible output and can check all of them.

Instant, offline, never fails, completely predictable. The boring answer is simply better
here.

## 8. Two things you asked about

### 8.1 "Why store anything at all? Can't everything live in the browser until signup?"

Some of it can, but most of it genuinely cannot — and the reason is that a conversation
has two people in it.

**What the browser holds:** a small token that says *"I am Quiet Otter,"* plus your theme
preference. That's it. Think of it as a key, not a filing cabinet.

**Why the rest has to live on our side:**

- **The other person's messages have to reach you.** If someone writes to you while you're
  offline, that message has to wait somewhere. It cannot wait in your browser, because
  your browser isn't there. This is the one that settles it on its own.
- **A friendship is two-sided.** If you add someone, they have to see you in *their*
  friends list. That's a fact about the pair of you, not a note in your browser.
- **Online status only works if we know.** To show your friends that you're around, we
  have to know you're around.
- **Names have to be unique.** You asked for shuffling that never hands out a name someone
  else is using. Two browsers can't check with each other; only a shared list can.
- **Matching needs both sides.** To pair you with someone who likes what you like, we need
  to know what you *both* like at the same moment.

So "anonymous" here means **we don't know who you are**, not **we store nothing**. There's
an account with a name, interests, friends and messages — it just has no email, no phone
number, and no real name attached. Signing up doesn't create an account; it attaches an
email to the one you already had, which is why nothing resets.

**And this is exactly why signing up matters.** The browser holds the only key to that
account. Lose the key and the account is still there — with your friends and your
conversations in it — but nobody can ever open it again, including you. Adding an email is
adding a second key.

### 8.2 "What's wrong with unlimited shuffling?"

Nothing — your version is better, and we'll do it your way.

You suggested unlimited shuffles, always handing out a name nobody's currently using, with
custom names reserved for people who sign up. That's a good design, and it adds a fourth
reason to sign up that we didn't have before (see §5, point 3).

The only things worth knowing, neither serious:

- **Some people will shuffle for a while**, effectively picking a username one tap at a
  time. That's mildly against the spirit of removing username-picking, but it's their time
  and it's one button. Not worth restricting.
- **A script could churn through names quickly**, so shuffling is capped at roughly one per
  second. Nobody tapping a button will ever notice; it just stops the pool being drained
  by something automated.

Beyond that, there's no catch.

## 9. What we're deliberately not building

Worth being explicit, so their absence reads as a decision rather than an oversight:

- **Browsing or searching for people** — you meet people by being matched, full stop
- **Group chats** — one-to-one only
- **Multiple conversations at once** — one at a time
- **Voice or video calls**
- **Phone notifications** — Shush only notifies you while it's open
- **Profile pictures, bios, or profiles at all** — your interests are your profile
- **A public launch to real strangers** — this runs with test users only

---

## 10. One thing to be aware of

This is being built as a demonstration project, running with test users. That's what makes
the anonymous-plus-photos combination safe to build.

If it were ever opened to the real public, anonymous strangers sending each other images
would need proper moderation and reporting infrastructure in place from day one — that's a
serious, separate piece of work, not a feature toggle. Worth knowing now so the decision is
never made casually later.
